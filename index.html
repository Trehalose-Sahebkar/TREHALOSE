Trehalose

A sugar built from two glucose units joined back to front. That single unusual bond explains almost everything it does.

Both anomeric carbons are tied up in the linkage, so trehalose has no free reducing end and no open-chain form.

Why the bond matters

In maltose, sucrose and lactose, at least one ring can open into a reactive aldehyde. Trehalose cannot. Joining the two glucoses through both of their anomeric carbons locks the molecule shut, and a locked molecule does not brown, does not caramelise easily, and does not attack the amino groups of proteins the way reducing sugars do.

The same closure makes trehalose unusually resistant to acid and heat, and gives it a glass transition temperature far above that of other common sugars. Dried down, it does not crystallise. It sets into an amorphous glass that traps whatever is suspended in it.

How it protects a drying cell

Tardigrades, brine shrimp cysts, baker's yeast and resurrection plants all accumulate trehalose before they dry out. Two ideas explain what it does, and they are not mutually exclusive.

Without a substitute, the hydrogen bonds that held the fold together are lost as water goes, and the protein does not come back.

Water replacement. The hydroxyls of trehalose hydrogen-bond to the polar groups on a protein surface in place of the departing water, so the fold never loses its scaffolding.

Vitrification. As the last water goes, trehalose forms a rigid glass. Molecular motion inside that glass slows to almost nothing, so unfolding cannot proceed on any relevant timescale.

This is why trehalose has been a standard excipient in freeze-dried biologics for decades, long before anyone proposed giving it as a drug.

What it does inside a human cell

The therapeutic interest rests on a second, quite separate property: trehalose induces autophagy, and it does so without inhibiting mTOR directly.

The mTOR-independent route is what made trehalose attractive. Rapamycin and its analogues also induce autophagy, but by suppressing mTOR they suppress protein synthesis and immune function along with it. A compound that reaches the same endpoint from a different direction avoids that cost, at least in principle.

Not all of this is settled. Work published since 2024 has argued that the autophagy-centric account is incomplete, and that some of what trehalose does in the gut is mediated by shifts in the microbiota rather than by anything happening inside host cells.

Where it is being tested

The clinical programme follows the mechanism: conditions where something needs clearing, or where a protein is misfolding.

Neurodegeneration phase 2 and earlier

Huntington's disease, spinocerebellar ataxia, ALS and tauopathies. Preclinical clearance of mutant huntingtin and tau is reproducible; the human trials so far have been small, and oral dosing runs straight into the bioavailability problem below.

Cardiovascular early clinical

Intravenous trehalose has been given weekly to patients with coronary disease, on the rationale that autophagic clearance of plaque macrophages limits lesion progression. The signal is interesting and the sample sizes are small.

Fatty liver and metabolic disease preclinical, some human data

Trehalose reduces hepatic steatosis in rodents through TFEB-driven lipophagy. Because intestinal trehalase splits most of an oral dose into glucose, human studies have to reckon with a glycaemic load that partly opposes the intended effect.

Dry eye approved in some markets

The one setting where the desiccation biology is used directly. Trehalose eye drops protect the corneal epithelium from drying stress, and are sold as a medical device or ophthalmic preparation in several countries.

Critical illness investigator-led trials

Supplementation in intensive care, measuring inflammatory and oxidative stress markers rather than hard clinical endpoints. Useful for establishing tolerability; not yet evidence of benefit.

The delivery problem

Almost every disappointing trehalose result traces back to the same place: very little of an oral dose survives to reach the tissue of interest.

Intranasal delivery is the route our own work is following, for a straightforward reason. Trehalase in the intestinal brush border removes most of an oral dose before absorption, and the blood–brain barrier removes most of what an intravenous dose delivers. Going through the nose avoids both.

The trade-off is volume. A nasal spray can deliver on the order of a hundred microlitres per nostril, so the total dose is measured in tens of milligrams rather than grams. Whether that is enough to reach a meaningful concentration in cerebrospinal fluid is an empirical question, and it is the question worth answering first.

What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Trehalose is frequently written about as though the preclinical case had already been settled in humans. It has not been.

Well established

Protein and membrane stabilisation during drying and freezing. This is engineering, not hypothesis, and it underpins the excipient use of trehalose in licensed biologics.

Not established

That oral trehalose meaningfully induces autophagy in human brain tissue. Bioavailability, not biology, is the limiting step.

Well established

Autophagy induction in cultured cells and in rodent models, at concentrations that are readily achieved in a dish.

Not established

A clinical benefit on any hard endpoint in neurodegenerative disease. Trials so far have been small, short, and mostly open-label.

Reasonably safe

Trehalose has GRAS status as a food ingredient and is well tolerated at gram-level oral doses, with osmotic diarrhoea as the main dose-limiting effect.

Open question

Whether dietary trehalose favours virulent Clostridioides difficile lineages, as work published in 2018 proposed. The finding remains contested and has not been resolved either way.
